# Is the Action Expert Low-Rank? Bytes-Matched Compression of a Flow-Matching VLA Under Closed-Loop Evaluation

Oct 28, 2026 · @Owen

## Project summary

**Central research question.** *Are the weights of a flow-matching VLA's action expert low-rank? And at matched bytes per denoising step, which compression method best preserves closed-loop task success?*

**Overview.** Flow-matching vision-language-action (VLA) models such as SmolVLA generate each action chunk in two phases. A vision-language prefix runs once per inference call. Then an action expert turns random noise into a chunk of future robot actions through about ten denoising passes. SmolVLA's action expert is a compact transformer of about 100M parameters that generates action chunks conditioned on the VLM's features \[1\]. It runs ten flow-matching steps at inference and outputs a 50-action chunk, but the LIBERO checkpoint is configured with n\_action\_steps = 1 \[12\]: in simulation the policy executes one action, re-observes, and denoises again, so the expert runs its full ten steps at every control tick and 49 of 50 predicted actions are discarded. The authors' ablation shows why: success falls from 80.3% at one executed step to 51.8% at fifty \[1\]. The execution horizon H is therefore an experimental axis, and every compressed model is compared against FP16 at the same H. We will:

1. measure the singular-value spectrum of every action-expert linear layer;
2. profile per-component latency at batch size 1 with a roofline analysis;
3. compare weight quantization, plain truncated SVD, importance-based (layer-selective) SVD, and a QSVD-style low-rank-plus-quantized hybrid \[3\] at matched bytes per denoising step, judged by closed-loop task success on LIBERO.

**Why the action expert is the target.** Every action chunk requires about ten sequential action-expert passes, so action generation dominates model latency and caps the achievable control frequency: SnapFlow reports the 10-step denoising chain at about 241 of 274 ms end-to-end for π0.5 on an A800 \[4\]. On a Hopper GPU at batch size 1, π0.5's action expert accounted for 8.8% of FLOPs but 57.2% of latency \[5\]. Each pass re-reads the expert's full weights while processing only about 50 action tokens. At batch size 1, the cost is therefore likely set by memory traffic and kernel-launch overhead rather than arithmetic, so shrinking the expert's weights targets its dominant cost.

**What is known.**

- **Quantization of the action expert works.** Earlier VLA post-training quantization kept the expert's attention projections in floating point and quantized only its MLP layers \[6\]\[7\]. CHASE-VLA quantizes both to W4A4 and reaches 97.3% average LIBERO success on π0.5, matching FP16, while cutting the expert's weight storage by 73.4% \[6\]. Those W4A4 results serve as our accuracy references; our latency baseline is weight-only quantization, for reasons given under Latency prediction.
- **Plain SVD is risky for VLAs, but the evidence comes from fine-tuned backbones.** GLUESTICK compared equal-sized layers in the language backbones of OpenVLA, LLaMA-2-7B and Qwen2-VL-7B. The VLA's singular values decay more slowly, with energy spread across many directions \[8\]. Because OpenVLA's backbone is LLaMA-2 fine-tuned on robot data, the same layers can be compared before and after: fine-tuning flattened both the language backbone (AUC/k 1.32 to 1.55) and the vision encoders (0.84 to 0.92) \[8\]. Replacing OpenVLA's weights with truncated SVD gave 0% LIBERO-Spatial success at ranks 200–1,000 and only 21% at rank 2,500 \[8\]. (An earlier draft also cited NestedVLA \[9\] for "no usable low-rank structure in fine-tuning deltas"; its abstract describes hypernetwork parameter generation for skill consolidation and we could not confirm that finding, so it is not relied on until the paper is read in full.)
- **Low-rank structure does exist elsewhere.** RL post-training produces low-rank updates concentrated in the action expert's timestep modules, while attention and MLP updates stay high-rank; that study measured weight *updates*, not weights \[10\]. The denoising velocity trajectory has a fast-decaying spectrum, which one paper exploits to cut ten denoising steps to two \[11\].
- **QSVD shows that rank allocation matters.** QSVD applies joint SVD to the query, key and value weights, allocates rank across layers by estimated loss impact, and adds quantization \[3\]. On SmolVLM-2B, plain ASVD fell to 0.69% ScienceQA accuracy at 80% of parameters kept, while QSVD without quantization kept 79.57% \[3\]. QSVD covers self-attention QKV only and names extending to all blocks as future work \[3\]. In SmolVLA's expert, self-attention Q, K and V share an input, so joint QKV applies directly. In cross-attention layers, Q takes action tokens while K and V take the VLM's cached keys and values respectively, so no two projections share an input and only per-matrix SVD or quantization applies. The gated MLP's gate and up projections do share an input, so a joint gate-up decomposition is the natural extension to the block that holds most of the bytes.

**Hypothesis.** All of this evidence comes from modules inherited from a pretrained LLM and then fine-tuned. SmolVLA's action expert is different: it is trained from scratch on about 23K robot episodes while the VLM stays frozen \[1\]. We ask whether a module trained only on narrow, smooth action data has more concentrated spectra than a fine-tuned backbone. If it does, the expert is a better SVD target than the backbone GLUESTICK studied; if it does not, flat spectra are not just a fine-tuning artifact, and quantization is favored. We expect a split inside the expert: cross-attention projections, which read VLM features, may stay flat, while self-attention and MLP layers may be steeper. As far as we found, prior spectral studies cover fine-tuned backbones \[8\], weight updates \[2\]\[10\], and denoising trajectories \[11\], but none has measured a flow-matching expert's trained weights or compared SVD, quantization, and hybrids at matched bytes in closed loop.

**Mechanism and a falsification test.** Concentrated spectra, if we find them, cannot come from the expert seeing only low-dimensional inputs: its input is a noisy action chunk, and even for a zero-variance action distribution the flow must remove noise in every dimension, so the expert's inputs are full-rank by construction. Any concentration would be a property of what training produced in the weights, not of the input subspace. Two controls keep the measurement honest. First, parameterization: for a gated MLP, W\_u' = DW\_u and W\_d' = W\_dD⁻¹ preserve the function for any invertible diagonal D while changing both matrices' singular values, so raw spectra are checkpoint-specific; we report spectra after a canonical rescaling (equalized per-channel norms) and after activation whitening as in ASVD, and show how much the conclusions move. Second, a layer split: Q and O projections and the MLP take action-token inputs in every layer, while cross-attention K/V projections take the VLM's cached key and value states, which carry the flat spectrum GLUESTICK found in fine-tuned backbones \[8\]. From the LeRobot configuration (to verify by counting), the MLP holds roughly 72% of expert bytes, Q and O about 22%, self-attention K/V about 4%, and cross-attention K/V about 2% (layers alternate between self- and cross-attention), so leaving cross-attention uncompressed costs almost nothing, and the MLP is where compression must work. Falsification: if cross-attention K/V layers show the same normalized AUC/k as self-attention layers, the input-source explanation is wrong.

**How the two families fail.** Truncation error ΔW = Σ\_{i>r} σᵢuᵢvᵢᵀ has rank n − r (540 for a 720×720 matrix at r = 180), so it is not low-rank; what is fixed is its row space, the dropped input-side directions. The output error x·ΔW is zero when x lies in the kept subspace and grows with the input's projection onto the dropped directions, so it is a systematic, input-dependent bias. Quantization error is full-rank with bounded, roughly independent entries: its output error is approximately isotropic noise scaled by ‖x‖, which hits dominant directions too but does not grow under distribution shift except through ‖x‖. The prediction is therefore that SVD degrades more than quantization on shifted inputs at matched bytes, and that a hybrid keeping the tail in low precision sits between them. We measure three open-loop diagnostics on recorded observations, in-distribution and LIBERO-Plus, with shared noise seeds and timestep schedule: per-step velocity cosine similarity to FP16, per-step relative L2 velocity error, and the integrated action-chunk error after all denoising steps, where Euler compounding shows. SVD should show error concentrated in a low-dimensional subspace that rises on shifted inputs; quantization should show roughly isotropic, shift-invariant error. Per-step velocity error is not what the robot sees. For an Euler sampler the terminal error is approximately δx\_N ≈ Σ\_k Φ\_{N,k+1} h e\_k, where Φ transports a step-k error through the remaining steps via the teacher's state Jacobians (computed with Jacobian-vector products along the teacher trajectory), and the controller executes only P\_H δx\_N. Writing q\_k = P\_H Φ\_{N,k+1} h e\_k, ‖Σ q\_k‖² = Σ‖q\_k‖² + 2Σ\_{i\<j}⟨q\_i, q\_j⟩: average per-step MSE omits both the transport and the cross terms, so a compressor with smaller local error can produce larger executed error if its transported errors reinforce. We therefore report a coherence index on transported, prefix-projected errors, C = ‖Σ\_k q\_k‖ / Σ\_k ‖q\_k‖ (1 for aligned errors, about 1/√K for uncorrelated ones), and the log-log slope of the free-running error against step. A toy velocity field (gated MLP, 10 Euler steps, matched bytes) gave C ≈ 0.94 and slope ≈ 1.06 for both families: along a smooth trajectory the input changes little between steps, so quantization's error, though random in weight space, stays as aligned as SVD's. These are toy numbers: one random gated-MLP block with imposed singular-value spectra, no attention, no timestep conditioning, and a synthetic shift, so they show the mechanism is plausible, not what SmolVLA's expert will do. Attention, nonlinearities and timestep conditioning may decorrelate errors across steps; if the real expert gives C well below 0.9 for quantization, the random-walk case returns. Our working expectation is that both compound roughly linearly, and we treat the spectrum's tail energy σ\_{r+1…} as the quantity that predicts damage: on the toy field, SVD at matched bytes was 6× worse than 4-bit weights under a flat spectrum and 2× better under a steep one, with shifted inputs hurting SVD only modestly more (18% vs 9%). These diagnostics explain closed-loop results; they do not replace them.

**Corrective behavior (hypothesis).** Compression may damage recovery before it visibly damages nominal action prediction. Demonstrations mostly show states near the intended trajectory, so directions that matter only after a slip, a missed grasp or a slightly wrong pose contribute little to nominal reconstruction loss, and calibration on nominal states underweights them. Two policies can agree exactly at the setpoint and still have opposite restoring responses after a small displacement. The question is not whether such directions exist in the weights but whether calibration discards them. It does not automatically favor a low-precision residual: on a 720×720 layer a 4-bit residual costs 259,200 B, the same as 90 additional bf16 ranks, so a few identifiable corrective directions favor extra ranks while many diffuse ones favor the residual. We measure this directly by projecting the teacher's change in action under perturbation onto each layer's singular basis and locating its energy relative to the truncation rank. Protocol: LIBERO-ReAct mid-execution perturbations \[31\] (action noise, gripper flips, end-effector displacement over a 20-step window), first confirming the FP16 teacher recovers; then paired rollouts with shared seeds for compressors chosen to have nominal velocity error within 10% of each other at matched bytes, reporting success, recovery time, excess degradation (perturbed minus unperturbed, per policy), and the teacher-forced action-response gap ‖(â\_pert − â\_nom) − (a\_pert − a\_nom)‖. Falsified if excess degradation tracks nominal error or does not differ between compressors, or if the corrective response lies mostly in the kept subspace. A rank-allocation criterion scored on perturbed states would count as a method only if it beats plain action distillation on the same perturbed states at equal bytes and calibration budget; matching the teacher's response to perturbation is otherwise Sobolev-style Jacobian matching \[33\] and DART-style state-noise distillation \[34\], not new.

**A sharper question: is the action expert low-rank, and where?** We first compute singular-value spectra for every action-expert linear layer: self-attention, cross-attention to VLM features, MLP, and the action/time input projections. We summarize each layer by the rank needed to capture 90% of its energy and by GLUESTICK's AUC/k flatness score, and compare against the VLM's layers and GLUESTICK's fine-tuned backbones \[8\]. We then compress at fixed byte budgets, comparing four variants: weight-only INT8/INT4 quantization (W8A16/W4A16; one fused dequantize-and-GEMM per layer); truncated SVD at rank r (two GEMMs); a QSVD-style hybrid that truncates and then quantizes the factors, with 4rE parameters at b bits, so 4rE·b/8 bytes, for a joint E×3E decomposition; and an SVDQuant-style hybrid that keeps a 16-bit low-rank branch plus a low-bit residual. For a 720×720 layer, INT8 costs 518,400 B, and bf16 SVD costs r·(720+720)·2 B, which matches at r = 180. Only the last variant keeps the low-energy tail, so it is the one the robustness hypothesis tests. Each compression variant is evaluated in closed loop, because compression error compounds across denoising steps and across executed actions. Both outcomes are informative: a hybrid or layer-selective method that beats quantization, or a clean, explained negative result that action experts should be quantized rather than factorized.

**Latency prediction.** At matched bytes the bandwidth term is equal by construction, so pure SVD can at best tie quantization on latency. It pays one extra kernel launch per factorized layer plus a small intermediate write (50 × r values). SmolVLA's expert has about 112 linear layers (16 layers × 7 projections) run over 10 steps, so roughly 1,000 extra launches: several milliseconds in eager PyTorch, 1–2 ms under CUDA graphs, against a bandwidth floor of about 7 ms on Thor (2 GB ÷ 273 GB/s). We therefore predict pure SVD loses on latency and can win only on success or robustness. Weight-only quantization is the latency baseline: at batch size 1 with 50 action tokens, a 720×720 layer's activations are about 72 KB per step against 1 MB of bf16 weights, so quantizing activations saves almost no bandwidth and adds a launch per layer; W4A4 results from CHASE-VLA \[6\] and Ω-QVLA \[27\] remain accuracy references at the same weight bytes. Quantization has the mirror risk: without a fused low-bit GEMM, dequantization writes a full bf16 copy and moves more bytes than it saves. The SVDQuant-style hybrid is the most expensive variant in eager PyTorch: two low-rank GEMMs plus one low-bit GEMM, with the addition fused or paid as a fourth launch. SVDQuant's own engine fuses all of this into one kernel; without a fused CUDA/CUTLASS or Triton kernel we expect it to have the worst wall-clock latency, so it is included for the robustness test, not as a speed candidate. At matched bytes its residual must be at most 4-bit, since a 4-bit residual plus a 16-bit rank-32 branch already costs 36% more than INT4 on a 720×720 layer. Both methods are measured with their best available kernels and under CUDA graphs. The launch penalty is fixed per layer while bytes scale with layer size, so it matters most on SmolVLA and shrinks on π0.5 and GR00T. For the expert's MLP block (E = 720, F = 2048) the arithmetic gives: W4A16 reads 2.28 MB per step; bf16 SVD factors cost 2(2E + 3F) bytes per unit of rank, so bytes match at rank 150; two extra launches at an assumed 2 µs (to measure on Thor) cost the equivalent of 1.09 MB at 273 GB/s, so SVD is strictly faster than W4A16 only below rank 78 (about 0.11E), and at no rank in eager PyTorch where launches cost about 8 µs. The expert's cross-attention K/V projections of the fixed VLM cache are identical at every step, so all baselines compute them once per call rather than per step.

**Value.**

- **A deployment answer:** which compression method to use for the component that dominates edge latency.
- **A spectral characterization of trained flow-matching action-expert weights,** compared directly against GLUESTICK's fine-tuned backbones \[8\].
- **An extension of QSVD \[3\] to the flow-matching expert:** joint gate-up decomposition for the MLP alongside joint QKV for self-attention, with its gradient-based rank allocation tested for a specific failure: global ranking can starve flat-spectrum layers, because their importance is spread across many components that each score low. The measured spectrum decides which layers are factorized at all; rank allocation acts only among those. The importance scores need gradients of a loss, which for an action expert is the flow-matching loss on demonstrations, not a text or image objective: we use a fixed, small calibration set of LIBERO expert demonstrations (on the order of QSVD's 256 samples), drawn from suites other than the one evaluated, with scores averaged over several noise and timestep draws because the loss is stochastic. Scores are also computed per denoising-step group ({0–2}, {3–6}, {7–9}, following CHASE-VLA \[6\]) to test whether rank importance is step-dependent: the gradient term in the score depends on the step while the singular values do not. In text-to-image diffusion, cross-attention converges in early steps while self-attention dominates later \[24\]; whether this holds for an action expert is untested. In SmolVLA, cross-attention K/V take the fixed VLM cache, so any step dependence enters through Q and the attention pattern. If the singular-value ranking changes across groups, a τ-averaged score hides it and a step schedule has something to exploit. Because the checkpoint executes one action per chunk, we also calibrate against the executed-prefix loss L\_H = E\_{c,z}‖P\_H F\_θ̂(c,z) − P\_H F\_θ(c,z)‖², with shared conditioning and noise, consistent action scaling, and padded output dimensions excluded. At the unchanged teacher this reconstruction loss has zero first derivative, so unlike QSVD's task-loss score it cannot rank deletions by gradient; we use finite deletions of small singular-value groups scored per byte saved, re-scored as compression proceeds, or a Gauss–Newton approximation. Unexecuted tokens are kept: cross-attention and shared weights couple them to the executed one. The test is whether prefix calibration beats full-chunk and activation-aware calibration on held-out closed-loop success at the deployed H; if it does not, this remains a diagnostic result. A further diagnostic, by paired finite differences, is whether compression changes the policy's sensitivity to the initial noise z differently from its sensitivity to the conditioning c, without treating low seed sensitivity as a goal, since a multimodal policy may legitimately vary across seeds.
- **A reusable profiling and evaluation harness.**

**Model choice.**

- **SmolVLA (0.45B, primary).** It has a LIBERO-finetuned checkpoint in LeRobot \[12\], and its design was optimized for small size and low latency \[1\]. It is not saturated on LIBERO: its authors report 87.3% with 10 trials per task \[1\], and an independent re-evaluation found 74.0% \[14\]. The 88.8% listed in some comparison tables \[13\] matches the authors' 2.25B variant (88.75%), not this checkpoint. Compression damage will therefore be visible.
- **π0.5 and GR00T N1.6 (extension).** Both pair a VLM with a flow-matching action expert \[6\]\[15\]. π0.5's expert has 18 blocks and runs 10 denoising steps on 50-action chunks; GR00T N1.6's DiT expert has 32 blocks and runs 4 steps on 16-action chunks \[6\]. Their experts are roughly 430M and 1.4B parameters (to verify by counting). Both score 97–98% on LIBERO in FP16 \[6\], so the extension relies on LIBERO-Plus and LIBERO-PRO to expose damage. They test whether the SmolVLA findings scale and hold across expert designs.
- **LIBERO.** A simulated benchmark of 130 Franka Panda manipulation tasks built on robosuite/MuJoCo \[16\]. Actions are 7-dimensional: a 6-DoF end-effector delta plus a gripper command (control rate to verify in the LIBERO configuration). The standard protocol is 10 tasks × 50 episodes per suite \[18\]. We use subsets for sweeps and full suites for final configurations. Standard LIBERO is not sufficient on its own: PIVOT-Q \[26\] showed that QuantVLA drops GR00T-N1.5 on LIBERO-Plus from 82.1% to 67.0% while standard LIBERO stays at 97%. LIBERO-Plus is therefore our primary robustness measure, because its seven graded perturbations give nonzero baselines and compare directly with PIVOT-Q. LIBERO-PRO's object and semantic perturbations are secondary, used only where the uncompressed model scores above zero; its position and task shifts drive π0.5 to 0.08–0.38 and π0 to 0, so SmolVLA would likely sit at a floor where compression cannot move the number. The robustness hypothesis is two-sided: rank truncation deletes low-energy directions, while quantization perturbs every direction slightly, so shifted inputs that excite rare directions should hurt SVD more; a hybrid that keeps the tail in low precision should sit between them.

**Hardware: A100 now, Jetson AGX Thor later.** Experiments run on NYU HPC Cloud Bursting A100 40GB GPUs (300 GPU-hours). These are real batch-1 measurements, but on datacenter hardware.

- **Edge devices differ by orders of magnitude.** The Jetson Orin NX delivers 4.8 FP16 TFLOPs, versus 4,500 for a B200 \[8\]. Edge memory bandwidth is also far lower: 273 GB/s on Jetson AGX Thor and 204.8 GB/s on AGX Orin \[19\], versus about 1.5 TB/s on an A100 (A100 figure to verify).
- **The bottleneck mix shifts.** Byte savings that barely register on an A100 may matter much more on robot hardware. For example, 2:4-sparse pruning delivered 1.8–1.9× end-to-end speedups on Jetson Thor \[8\].
- **What we report.** Measured A100 latency, plus projected edge latency (bytes moved ÷ device bandwidth) as a lower bound.
- **Extension.** Jetson AGX Thor T4000: 64 GB at 273 GB/s, the same bandwidth as the T5000 dev kit \[19\]. All three models fit. Published TensorRT pipelines on Thor serve as latency baselines \[17\]. An AGX Orin dev kit can be flashed to emulate Orin NX and Nano modules \[20\], giving a lower tier if one is available; the Orin NX leaves only about 13 GB usable \[8\].

**Risks.**

- **Headless rendering.** LIBERO renders through MuJoCo, which needs EGL on headless cluster nodes. Week 1 verifies one episode inside the Singularity container. Fallbacks are CPU rendering (OSMesa), or open-loop action error on recorded demonstrations (weaker evidence).
- **Launch overhead may hide speedups on the A100.** We establish a CUDA-graphs baseline before measuring compression, and report bytes moved alongside wall-clock time.
- **Quantization may save memory but not time** without real low-bit kernels. SVD's speedups are real in standard PyTorch.
- **Plain SVD may collapse, as GLUESTICK's backbone results suggest \[8\].** A collapse in a from-scratch expert is itself a finding: flat spectra would then not be only a fine-tuning artifact. The hybrid and importance-based variants remain the positive path.
- **LIBERO variance.** We use fixed seeds and initial states, and report confidence intervals.
- **Baseline drift.** Published SmolVLA success varies: 87.3% from its authors with 10 trials per task \[1\], versus 74.0% in an independent re-evaluation \[14\]. We reproduce our own baseline first, at 50 episodes per task.
- **Rank allocation may starve flat layers.** QSVD's global importance ranking can under-allocate rank to layers whose spectrum is flat. Mitigation: spectrum-gated layer selection, then a per-layer minimum rank.

## Novelty check (Oct 29, 2026)

Of the four candidate contributions, two are still open (weight spectra; matched-bytes SVD vs quantization vs hybrid) and two are largely done (robustness erosion; Thor speedups). The defensible paper merges them into one question: **do low-rank and low-bit compression of the action expert fail differently, both in robustness under distribution shift and in real latency on Thor?** Nothing found answers that.

**5. Step-scheduled rank (added Nov 5).** Status: done for image DiTs, open for VLA action experts. SARA \[21\] adapts the SVD rank of pretrained linear layers per step and block at inference; SlimDiff \[22\] allocates rank from timestep-stratified activation statistics; AdaTSQ \[23\] schedules activation bit-width per step. What is left: apply step-scheduled rank to a flow-matching expert in closed loop. The structural point: with U, Σ, V stored once at r\_max, reading the first r\_t columns at step t is a contiguous slice that any GEMM can consume, so rank is a native runtime knob; weight bit-width can be made one too, but only with bit-plane storage and a custom kernel (Any-Precision LLM \[25\]). The matched-bytes test is a schedule with mean rank r̄ against fixed rank r̄: same bytes and launches per call, with the footprint growing to r\_max. The schedule's direction is measured by per-step sensitivity, not assumed; the per-step importance scores in the Value section are the cheap precursor. Two budgets are possible: every step individually faster than W4A16 (each r\_t below the crossover rank), or only the per-call total faster, which lets late steps exceed the cap if early ones fall below it.

**6. Execution horizon and transported error (added Nov 5).** Status: open as a combination; each piece has a neighbor. CTEC \[28\] handles cross-timestep error propagation in quantized image diffusion. Traj-MC \[29\] calibrates low-rank compression on trajectory states using activation second moments, but for diffusion language models. QCRL \[30\] recovers quantized VLAs with offline RL from full-precision rollouts; its W4A16 π0.5 on Jetson Thor reaches 99.3% of FP success, and its NVFP4 variant 1.73× speedup, which are latency and recovery baselines for us. None studies how the execution horizon H changes which compression wins, calibrates to the executed prefix, or measures transported-error alignment in a VLA. The honest objection: at H = 1 the cheapest latency lever is executing more of the chunk or cutting denoising steps, not compressing weights, so the proposal must show compression composes with those and evaluate at least two horizons. The falsifier is that prefix calibration does not beat full-chunk and activation-aware calibration on held-out closed-loop success; then the contribution is diagnostic. Recovery under mid-execution perturbation is evaluated by OGPO's LIBERO-ReAct \[31\] (π0.5 robustness 76.7% for SFT, 81.8% with OGPO) and CoRe \[32\], but neither compresses the policy; whether compression damages recovery at matched nominal error appears untested.

PIVOT-Q also strengthens the motivation: compression damage hides under distribution shift, and no one has checked whether low-rank compression hides it the same way.

## Out of scope

The project compares low-rank, low-bit and hybrid compression of the action expert on SmolVLA, π0.5 and GR00T N1.6, evaluated on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus and LIBERO-PRO, with latency on Jetson AGX Thor (T4000). Everything below is excluded or used only as a baseline.

| Excluded | Why | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thor latency measurement as a contribution | Jetson AI Lab and FlashRT already publish it | Baselines only |
| A new W4A4 quantizer | CHASE-VLA and Ω-QVLA already reach FP16-level success on π0.5 | Baselines only |
| Quantization-only robustness study; robustness-recovery fine-tuning | PIVOT-Q covers both | Cite and position against |
| π0 | Superseded by π0.5, same design | Dropped |
| π0.7, Gemini Robotics 2 | Weights are closed | Dropped |
| GR00T N1.7 | N1.6 has the larger expert and CHASE-VLA baselines | Possible extension |
| Latency-injected standard LIBERO as headline evidence | [VLASH](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.01031): π0.5 drops only 96.8% to 94.6% at a 3-step delay | Sanity check only; dynamic tasks ([ReflexBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14379), DOM) if time allows |
| Non-NVIDIA edge hardware (Qualcomm, Intel, Ascend) | Needs porting off CUDA | Future work |
| Training or fine-tuning on the Jetson | Thor is an inference target | Train on NYU HPC A100s |
| Real-robot experiments | No robot access for the course | Future work |

Also excluded as a contribution: rotation-based outlier smoothing. Ω-QVLA applies an SVD-Hadamard rotation to the full DiT head and QSVD adopts QuaRot's rotation, so rotation is a baseline component, not a differentiator.

Also excluded from the course deliverable: step-scheduled rank, which needs per-step sensitivity sweeps on top of the matched-bytes grid. It is a spring extension; the per-step importance scores are its precursor. Likewise a full sweep over the execution horizon H: the course evaluates two horizons (the deployed H = 1 and one longer), and a full H-versus-compression trade-off is extension work.
